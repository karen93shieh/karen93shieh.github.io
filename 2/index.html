Part 1 — Fun with Filters

1.1 Convolutions from Scratch

I implemented 2D convolution with: (a) four nested loops, (b) two loops with numpy vectorized inner operations, and (c) compared against scipy.signal.convolve2d. I used zero padding. Below is a snippet for the looped version. 

# 4 loops (numpy)
def conv_4(img, kernel):
    h, w = img.shape
    kh, kw = kernel.shape
    pad_h = kh // 2 
    pad_w = kw // 2
    padded_img = np.pad(img, ((pad_h,pad_h),(pad_w,pad_w)), mode="constant", constant_values=0)
    kernel = np.flip(kernel)
    out = np.zeros(img.shape)
    if kernel.sum() != 0:
        kernel = kernel / kernel.sum()         
    for i in range(h):
        for j in range(w):
            for k in range(kh):
                for l in range(kw):
                    out[i][j] += padded_img[i+k][j+l] * kernel[k][l]             
    return out 
# 2 loops (numpy)
def conv_2(img, kernel):
    h, w = img.shape
    kh, kw = kernel.shape
    pad_h = kh // 2 
    pad_w = kw // 2
    padded_img = np.pad(img, ((pad_h,pad_h),(pad_w,pad_w)), mode="constant", constant_values=0)
    kernel = np.flip(kernel)
    out = np.zeros(img.shape)
    if kernel.sum() != 0:
        kernel = kernel / kernel.sum()  
    for i in range(h):
        for j in range(w):
            mask = padded_img[i:i+kh, j:j+kw]
            out[i][j] = np.sum(mask * kernel)    
    return out

1.2 Finite Difference Operator

Using Dx and Dy, I computed the edges, gradient magnitude, and a binarized edge map (threshold tuned qualitatively).

The gradient magnitude was calculated by taking the sum of square of the dx and dy images.

The threshold was selected as keeping the 95.75 percentile, keeping anything that's above that. That is, keep the top 5% of values in the min-max normalized gradient image. The trade-off is that with a smaller threshaold, we see more noise in the background (buildings). With a bigger threshold, however, causes us to lose details on the cameraman.

1.3 Derivative of Gaussian (DoG)

To suppress noise, I blurred with a Gaussian before differencing, and also built DoG filters directly by convolving the Gaussian with Dx/Dy and applying the resulting filters to the image—both paths matched numerically.

The difference of blurring with a Gaussian is obvious, the noise are dimmed (say, the grass mostly disappear on the gradient magnitde image) and the real edges are thicker.

We can see that there is no difference in the images where we convolve the images with the gaussian and the dx, dy, and the images that we directly convolve the gaussian with the dx, dy, and then apply to the images.

Part 2 — Fun with Frequencies

2.1 Image “Sharpening” (Unsharp Mask)

I implemented unsharp masking by adding scaled high frequencies back to the image. I also blurred a sharp image and attempted to re-sharpen, comparing to the original.

To blur the image is to remove high frequeny from it. That means if we take the original image, and remove (subtract) the blurred image (low frequency), what we have left is the high frequency. Why does it matter? Because the high frequencies are the details, the sharp edges, that can be used to sharpen the original image. This proccess is called "unsharpening."

Below are some images that went throuh this process. One can see that the heavier the weight of the edges is, the more "pop out" the details are.

2.2 Hybrid Images

I aligned pairs, low-pass filtered one image and high-pass filtered the other, and combined them. For my favorite hybrid, I visualized the Fourier spectra along the pipeline.

I used the dog and cat images to plot out their grayscales, as well as visualizing the Fourier graph. The cutoff was chosen to be a gaussian with kernel size of 2 and sigma of 4 as the blurred value of the dog. As for the high frequency, the cutoff is a gaussian with kernel size of 49 and a sigma of 8, then take the original image minus the blurred image. The high frequency cat then are "sharpen" by a factor of 3 (so the edges are more obvious)

2.3 Gaussian & Laplacian Stacks

I built Gaussian and Laplacian stacks (no downsampling as opposed to pyramid) and reproduced the Oraple figure structure, as well as other images.

For the oraple, I created a Gaussian/Laplacian stack with 8 layers. Then, I applied the "binary" masks (that went through the same Gaussian filter) to the apple and orange's Laplacian stacks, individually. The masks were "flipped" (1-value) so that the images can be sticth together in the middle.

2.4 Multiresolution Blending (a.k.a. the oraple!)

Continue on part 2.3, after applying the mask, I added all the images together, as well as the final layer of the Gaussian (since the Laplacian is a difference between the Gaussians, which means the lowest frequency information is missing if we simply just add the Laplacian without the final blurred layer of the Gaussian).

I searched through different images to see what would align the best together, and found that things that have similar general shape (say, apples and oranges) works pretty well togheter. So I chose blending a banana and a cucumber, as well as cars (since on car websites the images for cars are identical, except for the color change).
